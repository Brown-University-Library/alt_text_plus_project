## Selection standard

Every result below had to meet all of these conditions:

1. The link resolves to a specific image, not just a search page.
2. The image's museum record is explicitly marked CC0.
3. The exact published description is attached to that specific primary image and is used as its screen-reader-accessible alt text.
4. The image's published metadata says `human_reviewed: true`.
5. The description stands on visual content rather than depending on an article, lesson, or other surrounding context to make sense.
6. The normalized description is no more than 200 characters, treating 200 as an inclusive maximum.

The descriptions are reproduced exactly apart from collapsing line breaks and repeated spaces. All 12 entries from the earlier report that met the new limit were retained; 38 longer entries were replaced with other qualifying records.

## How character counts were calculated

Each count includes every displayed letter, number, space, and punctuation mark in the reproduced alt text. Before counting, line breaks and repeated whitespace were collapsed to single spaces, and whitespace at the beginning or end was removed. The quoted text and the count therefore correspond to each other. The maximum for this sample is 200 characters inclusive.

## Why this source qualifies

All 50 results come from the Cleveland Museum of Art's image-description initiative. This is stronger evidence than finding ordinary alt text on a museum website:

- The museum developed its standard with [Prime Access Consulting](https://www.pac.bz/), an accessibility firm led by disabled experts.
- The museum and the accessibility consultants spent months defining a high-quality visual description and produced a [collection-specific style guide](https://static.sched.com/hosted_files/mcn2024/15/CMA%20Collection%20Online%20Alt-Text%20Style%20Guide.pdf).
- Museum staff first wrote descriptions themselves and refined them in response to the consultants' feedback.
- The publishing system distinguishes descriptions that received human review from descriptions that did not. This report includes only records whose primary-image metadata says `human_reviewed: true`.
- The descriptions are attached to specific images, rather than merely to artwork records, and the museum uses them as screen-reader alt text.

This does not mean that every reader will prefer every wording. It means there is documented, accessibility-informed human judgment behind the selected descriptions—the standard requested for this search.

## License and reuse

Every direct image link below is from the museum's Open Access image service, and every corresponding record reports `share_license_status: "CC0"`. The museum says its CC0 images may be downloaded, shared, changed, and reused without asking permission. Attribution is not legally required for CC0 material, but retaining the museum record link is useful for provenance.

CC0 addresses copyright. A user should still consider non-copyright issues that may apply to a particular use, such as privacy, publicity, cultural sensitivity, or trademarks.

All direct image and museum-record links were checked on 2026-08-12 and returned successfully.

## Research notes and exclusions

The revised search inspected 362 primary images whose published metadata identified them as human-reviewed. Sixty-two of those descriptions met the 200-character maximum; 50 were selected for variety while preserving all qualifying entries from the earlier report.

Web searches also identified several potentially useful sources that were not used for the final 50:

- [Cooper Hewitt's Guidelines for Image Description](https://www.cooperhewitt.org/cooper-hewitt-guidelines-for-image-description/) supplies unusually strong, specialist-written examples. However, many of its example images are modern copyrighted works or site assets without a clearly stated CC0/CC BY license.
- [DIAGRAM Center's Image Description Guidelines](https://diagramcenter.org/table-of-contents-2.html) are grounded in accessibility research and include many image types. Many underlying examples, however, are copyrighted textbook figures, and several demonstrate context-dependent descriptions—the kind the prompt asked to avoid.
- The expert-rated [Composing alt text using large language models](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/szh5zhpgxh/1) dataset explicitly scores descriptions for usefulness, reliability, completeness, accuracy, and literacy. Most original images are copyrighted press photographs or protected logos, so they did not meet the reuse requirement.
- Ordinary museum alt text, web-scraped caption datasets, and descriptions that passed only an automated score were excluded.

The Cleveland source ultimately provided the clearest intersection of accessibility expertise, explicit human review, context-independent visual description, individual image links, clear CC0 status, and enough variety for a balanced set of 50.

- [Cleveland Museum of Art Open Access](https://www.clevelandart.org/open-access) — explains CC0 reuse and how Open Access records are identified.
- [Cleveland Museum of Art Open Access API](https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/) — documents the collection fields and CC0 filter.
- [CMA Collection Online Alt-Text Style Guide](https://static.sched.com/hosted_files/mcn2024/15/CMA%20Collection%20Online%20Alt-Text%20Style%20Guide.pdf) — the collection-specific writing standard.

- [DIAGRAM Center Image Description Guidelines](https://diagramcenter.org/table-of-contents-2.html) — an accessibility-research source consulted for non-art image types.

## Clarifications supplied

Acceptable rights: CC0/public domain or CC BY with attribution. Merely appearing on a GLAM+ organization's website was not treated as proof of quality. Preference was given to sources explicitly concerned with producing good alt text, and a shared quality reason could apply to multiple links from the same source.
